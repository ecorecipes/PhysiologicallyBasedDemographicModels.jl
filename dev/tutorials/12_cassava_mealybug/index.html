Background

The cassava mealybug Phenacoccus manihoti Matile-Ferrero was accidentally introduced from South America to Africa in the early 1970s, where it rapidly became the most devastating pest of cassava (Manihot esculenta) — the primary staple food crop for over 200 million people across sub-Saharan Africa. Without natural enemies in its new range, mealybug populations exploded during dry seasons, causing severe shoot tip bunching, leaf curling, defoliation, and tuber yield losses estimated at 50–80% in heavily infested fields.

In what became one of the most successful classical biological control programs in history, the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) imported the solitary encyrtid parasitoid Epidinocarsis lopezi (De Santis) from South America and first released it in Nigeria in 1981. By 1988, E. lopezi had established in 19 African countries, spreading over 1.5 million km², and was credited with reducing mealybug populations to economically insignificant levels across the cassava belt. Neuenschwander et al. (1989) estimated yield gains of 2.5 t/ha in the savanna zone — translating to hundreds of millions of dollars annually in averted losses.

Gutierrez et al. (1988a,b) developed physiologically based demographic models of the cassava–mealybug–parasitoid tritrophic system, demonstrating that E. lopezi alone was capable of preventing tuber yield losses, while native coccinellid predators had comparatively small impact. The PBDM framework captured the critical role of temperature in mediating the interaction: both mealybug and parasitoid develop through physiological (degree-day) time, and the parasitoid’s slightly faster development rate at moderate temperatures gives it a competitive advantage that underpins successful biological control.

This vignette builds a tritrophic PBDM of the cassava–mealybug–parasitoid system using PhysiologicallyBasedDemographicModels.jl, with parameters drawn from Gutierrez et al. (1988a,b) and Neuenschwander et al. (1989).

The model follows the supply/demand structure of Gutierrez et al. (1988b): the mealybug feeds on the plant’s photosynthate through a Frazer–Gilbert functional response, and the ratio of supply to demand, φ, scales its fecundity and survival. The plant loses whatever the mealybug takes. E. lopezi attacks the mealybug through the parasitoid form of the same functional response. Insect parameters come from the Appendix of Gutierrez et al. (1988b, p. 940). The cassava plant is much simpler than the carbon, nitrogen and water model of Gutierrez et al. (1988a). Its parts that are not from the papers are listed in the parameter table at the end.

using Statistics
using CairoMakie

1. Cassava Plant Model

Cassava development is driven by thermal accumulation above a base temperature of 10 °C. The canopy is a distributed delay of leaf dry mass (g per plant) whose leaves age out after about 2500 degree-days. Each day the canopy intercepts light and produces photosynthate. The light model is the one in the CASAS cassava code (Gutierrez & Ponti, bio.pas): interception $1 - e^{-0.8047\,\mathrm{LAI}}$ enters a Frazer–Gilbert response whose demand is the plant’s maximum daily growth demand $b_p$, and solar radiation (cal cm⁻² d⁻¹) is converted to carbohydrate as radiation/3.875 (Loomis & Williams 1963). Leaf area per gram (1.80 dm² g⁻¹) and the planting density (one plant per m²) are taken from the same code. Photosynthate left after the mealybug has fed first replaces the leaves that aged out, holding the canopy at its initial size. The rest is stored in the tubers. The maximum demand $b_p$ has no published value here. It is set to 10 g CH₂O per plant per day, which gives roughly 3 kg of dry matter per plant per year, about 30 t ha⁻¹ at one plant per m².

# Cassava development parameters (Gutierrez et al. 1988a)
const CASSAVA_T_BASE  = 10.0   # °C
const CASSAVA_T_UPPER = 38.0   # °C
cassava_dev = LinearDevelopmentRate(CASSAVA_T_BASE, CASSAVA_T_UPPER)

# Light interception and photosynthesis (CASAS cassava code, bio.pas / init.pas)
const LEAF_AREA_PER_G = 1.80    # dm² leaf per g leaf (variety TMS30572)
const PLANT_AREA_DM2  = 100.0   # dm² ground per plant (1 m × 1 m spacing)
const B_PLANT = 10.0            # g CH₂O plant⁻¹ day⁻¹, maximum plant demand [assumed]

leaf_area_index(leaf_g) = leaf_g * LEAF_AREA_PER_G / PLANT_AREA_DM2

"""Daily photosynthate supply (g CH₂O per plant) from leaf mass and radiation (MJ m⁻² d⁻¹)."""
function photosynthate(leaf_g, radiation_MJ)
    α = 1 - exp(-0.8047 * leaf_area_index(leaf_g))
    α <= 0 && return 0.0
    light = radiation_MJ * 23.9 / 3.875 * PLANT_AREA_DM2 / 100   # g CH₂O equivalent
    return acquire(FraserGilbertResponse(α), light, B_PLANT)
end

# Canopy: 125 g of leaf per plant in 25 age classes (LAI ≈ 2.3)
make_cassava() = Population(:cassava,
    [LifeStage(:canopy, DistributedDelay(25, 2500.0; W0=5.0), cassava_dev, 0.0002)])

cassava = make_cassava()
println("Initial leaf mass: $(total_population(cassava)) g/plant, " *
        "LAI = $(round(leaf_area_index(total_population(cassava)), digits=2))")
for rad in (14.0, 18.0, 22.0)
    println("  radiation $rad MJ/m² → supply $(round(photosynthate(125.0, rad), digits=1)) g CH₂O/plant/day")
end

2. Cassava Mealybug (Phenacoccus manihoti)

P. manihoti is parthenogenetic, so every individual is female. The life cycle follows the Appendix of Gutierrez et al. (1988b): embryos develop inside the mother for 65 DD, eggs take 114 DD, immatures 192 DD, and adults live 371 DD, all above 13.5 °C.

Demand. The population’s demand rate $b$ (eqn 5) is the sum of immature growth, embryo growth, reserves (10 % of growth) and respiration, divided by $1-\beta$ for egestion ($\beta = 0.176$). Immature growth demand at age $a$ DD is $0.0249\,e^{-4.6 + 0.0249a}$ mg DD⁻¹, embryo growth demand is 0.000154 mg DD⁻¹, and respiration is $0.135\,e^{0.007\Delta t}$ × active weight per day (Appendix).

Fecundity. The maximum rate of embryo production by an adult of age $x$ DD (since adult emergence) is $0.47x/1.0072^x$ (Appendix). Read as embryos per female per day, this gives about 500 embryos over an adult life at 27 °C when food is unlimited. Births are this maximum scaled by φ (eqn 2).

# P. manihoti (Gutierrez et al. 1988b, Appendix p. 940)
const CM_T_BASE  = 13.5   # °C
const CM_T_UPPER = 35.0   # °C (Le Rü & Fabres 1987)
cm_dev = LinearDevelopmentRate(CM_T_BASE, CM_T_UPPER)

const CM_SEARCH = 0.25     # Frazer–Gilbert search rate on photosynthate
const CM_EGESTED = 0.176   # proportion egested, β
const CM_RESERVES = 0.10   # reserves, fraction of growth demand
const CM_EMBRYO_DEMAND = 0.000154   # mg per embryo per DD

cm_growth_demand(a) = 0.0249 * exp(-4.6 + 0.0249a)   # mg per immature per DD at age a
cm_mass(a) = exp(-4.6) * (exp(0.0249a) - 1)          # mg, integral of growth demand
const CM_ADULT_MASS = cm_mass(192.0)
cm_fecundity(x) = x <= 0 ? 0.0 : 0.47x / 1.0072^x     # embryos/female/day at age x DD

make_mealybug() = Population(:mealybug, [
    LifeStage(:embryo,   DistributedDelay(10, 65.0;  W0=0.0), cm_dev, 0.005),
    LifeStage(:egg,      DistributedDelay(10, 114.0; W0=2.0), cm_dev, 0.003),  # 20 eggs (1 ovisac) per plant
    LifeStage(:immature, DistributedDelay(20, 192.0; W0=0.0), cm_dev, 0.004),
    LifeStage(:adult,    DistributedDelay(15, 371.0; W0=0.0), cm_dev, 0.005),
])

"""Mealybug demand rate b (g per plant per day), eqn 5, given today's degree-days."""
function mealybug_demand(mb, dd)
    emb, imm, ad = mb.stages[1].delay, mb.stages[3].delay, mb.stages[4].delay
    age(d, j) = (j - 0.5) * d.τ / d.k
    growth = sum(imm.W[j] * cm_growth_demand(age(imm, j)) for j in 1:imm.k) * dd
    embryo = delay_total(emb) * CM_EMBRYO_DEMAND * dd
    active = sum(imm.W[j] * cm_mass(age(imm, j)) for j in 1:imm.k) +
             delay_total(ad) * CM_ADULT_MASS +
             sum(emb.W[j] * CM_EMBRYO_DEMAND * age(emb, j) for j in 1:emb.k)
    respiration = 0.135 * exp(0.007dd) * active
    return ((1 + CM_RESERVES) * (growth + embryo) + respiration) / (1 - CM_EGESTED) / 1000
end

"""Embryos initiated today at full nutrition."""
function mealybug_max_births(mb)
    ad = mb.stages[4].delay
    return sum(ad.W[j] * cm_fecundity((j - 0.5) * ad.τ / ad.k) for j in 1:ad.k)
end

let dd = 27.0 - CM_T_BASE
    days = 371 / dd
    eggs = sum(cm_fecundity(x) for x in range(0, 371; length=1000)) * 371 / 1000 / dd
    println("Adult life at 27 °C ≈ $(round(days, digits=1)) days; " *
            "maximum embryos per female ≈ $(round(eggs))")
end
Adult life at 27 °C ≈ 27.5 days; maximum embryos per female ≈ 503.0

3. Parasitoid (Epidinocarsis lopezi)

E. lopezi is a solitary endoparasitoid of P. manihoti. From the Appendix of Gutierrez et al. (1988b): the threshold is 13.3 °C, eggs and larvae take 100 DD, pupae 86 DD, and adults live 280 DD. Hyperparasitoids are found at 0.28 times the density of E. lopezi immatures (p. 926). Here that is read as 28 % of immatures lost over the 186 DD of immature development.

# E. lopezi (Gutierrez et al. 1988b, Appendix p. 940)
const EL_T_BASE  = 13.3   # °C
const EL_T_UPPER = 34.0   # °C [assumed]
el_dev = LinearDevelopmentRate(EL_T_BASE, EL_T_UPPER)

const EL_OVIPOSITION = 0.9     # eggs per female per DD (maximum demand)
const EL_HOST_FEEDING = 0.3    # proportion of attacked hosts used for host feeding
const EL_GAMMA = 0.05          # aggregate search S_E = 1 - exp(-γ FEM)
const EL_PREF_IMMATURE = 0.3   # host preferences
const EL_PREF_ADULT = 0.65
const EL_MU_HYPER = -log(1 - 0.28) / (100 + 86)   # per DD, hyperparasitism on immatures

el_oviposition_temperature(dd) = max(0.0, 1 - ((dd - 10) / 10)^2)   # Appendix

make_parasitoid() = Population(:parasitoid, [
    LifeStage(:egg_larva, DistributedDelay(15, 100.0; W0=0.0), el_dev, EL_MU_HYPER),
    LifeStage(:pupa,      DistributedDelay(10, 86.0;  W0=0.0), el_dev, EL_MU_HYPER),
    LifeStage(:adult,     DistributedDelay(10, 280.0; W0=0.0), el_dev, 0.006),
])

println("E. lopezi: egg–larva 100 DD, pupa 86 DD, adult 280 DD above $(EL_T_BASE) °C")
for T in (20.0, 23.3, 27.0, 30.0)
    dd = T - EL_T_BASE
    println("  T = $T °C: oviposition scalar $(round(el_oviposition_temperature(dd), digits=2)), " *
            "max eggs/female/day $(round(EL_OVIPOSITION * dd * el_oviposition_temperature(dd), digits=1))")
end

4. Trophic Interactions

Mealybug feeding (eqns 2–4). The mealybug population, with demand $b$, searches today’s photosynthate $P$ with search rate $s = 0.25$. It acquires $A = b\,(1 - e^{-sP/b})$ and φ = $A/b$. The plant loses $A$. φ scales embryo production (eqn 2) and survival (eqn 6, $l_{x\phi}$ = φ per day). The CASAS code applies φ to every mealybug stage and to the parasitoid immatures inside them, and so does this vignette.

Parasitism (eqns 7–13). Hosts are weighted by preference, $H_w = 0.3\,N_\mathrm{imm} + 0.65\,N_\mathrm{adult}$. The females’ demand for hosts is $b_E = 0.9\,\Delta t\,\phi_T\,\mathrm{FEM}/(1 - 0.3)$: oviposition, plus host feeding, which takes 0.3 of the hosts attacked. The females’ search is $S_E = 1 - e^{-0.05\,\mathrm{FEM}}$. In the parasitoid form of the Frazer–Gilbert model, $E = b_E(1 - e^{-S_E H_w/b_E})$ attacks are spread at random over the hosts, so $N_a = H_w(1 - e^{-E/H_w})$ distinct hosts are attacked. Each stage loses $\xi_i N_a / H_w$ of its members (eqns 9–12). Of the hosts attacked, 0.7 are parasitized, and the female fraction of the progeny is $\mathrm{SR} = H_w/H_T$, bounded to [0.3, 0.75] (eqn 13). The ratio of hosts attacked to demand, $N_a/b_E$, sets the daily survival (and emigration) of the adult parasitoids (p. 926).

cm_feeding = FraserGilbertResponse(CM_SEARCH)

"""Hosts attacked by FEM female E. lopezi on weighted host density Hw, with demand bE."""
function parasitoid_attacks(Hw, FEM, bE)
    (Hw <= 0 || FEM <= 0 || bE <= 0) && return 0.0
    SE = 1 - exp(-EL_GAMMA * FEM)
    eggs = bE * (1 - exp(-SE * Hw / bE))
    return Hw * (1 - exp(-eggs / Hw))
end

println("--- Mealybug supply/demand (P = 9.8 g CH₂O/plant/day) ---")
for n in (1_000, 2_000, 5_000, 10_000, 20_000)
    b = n * 0.4e-3     # ≈ 0.4 mg per mealybug per day
    println("  ≈$(lpad(n, 6)) mealybugs: demand $(round(b, digits=1)) g/day, " *
            "φ = $(round(supply_demand_ratio(cm_feeding, 9.8, b), digits=3))")
end

println("\n--- E. lopezi attacks per day at 27 °C ---")
let dd = 27.0 - EL_T_BASE
    for FEM in (1.0, 10.0, 100.0), Hw in (100.0, 1000.0, 10000.0)
        bE = EL_OVIPOSITION * dd * el_oviposition_temperature(dd) * FEM / (1 - EL_HOST_FEEDING)
        Na = parasitoid_attacks(Hw, FEM, bE)
        println("  FEM = $(lpad(FEM, 5)), Hw = $(lpad(Hw, 7)): hosts attacked $(round(Na, sigdigits=3)), " *
                "attacked/demand $(round(Na / bE, digits=2))")
    end
end

5. Weather: West African Savanna

We simulate three years of weather representative of the Nigerian/Ghanaian savanna zone where the mealybug problem was most severe: a warm tropical climate with a distinct dry season. Mean annual temperature is about 27 °C, with moderate seasonal variation. The weather has no rainfall, so the rain-induced mortality of Gutierrez et al. (1988b, eqn 16) does not arise.

const N_YEARS = 3
n_days = 365 * N_YEARS
savanna_T(d) = 27.0 + 3.0 * sin(2π * (d - 100) / 365)
savanna_rad(d) = 18.0 + 4.0 * sin(2π * (d - 60) / 365)   # MJ m⁻² d⁻¹, higher in the dry season

function savanna_weather(; offset=0.0, n=n_days)
    WeatherSeries([DailyWeather(savanna_T(d) + offset, savanna_T(d) + offset - 5.0,
                                savanna_T(d) + offset + 5.0;
                                radiation=savanna_rad(d), photoperiod=12.2) for d in 1:n];
                  day_offset=1)
end
weather = savanna_weather()

println("\n--- West African Savanna Weather ---")
for (label, d) in [("Jan (dry)", 15), ("Apr (onset)", 100),
                    ("Jul (wet)", 195), ("Oct (late)", 285)]
    w = get_weather(weather, d)
    println("  $label: T=$(round(w.T_mean, digits=1))°C, " *
            "Rad=$(round(w.radiation, digits=1)) MJ/m²")
end

6. The Coupled Model

The cassava canopy, tuber store, mealybug and parasitoid are components of a PopulationSystem. One CustomRule carries out each day’s trophic interactions after the populations have aged. It computes the plant’s photosynthate, the mealybug’s demand, acquisition and φ, plant growth, births, food-related survival, and parasitism.

"""
Build and solve the cassava–mealybug–E. lopezi system. Returns stage totals,
tuber mass and the daily log of the trophic rule.
"""
function simulate_tritrophic(weather, n_days;
                             introduce_parasitoid=true,
                             introduction_day=90,
                             initial_parasitoids=0.5)   # adult females per plant (Appendix)
    sys = PopulationSystem(
        :cassava    => PopulationComponent(make_cassava(); species=:cassava),
        :tuber      => PopulationComponent(BulkPopulation(:tuber, 0.0); species=:cassava),
        :mealybug   => PopulationComponent(make_mealybug(); species=:mealybug),
        :parasitoid => PopulationComponent(make_parasitoid(); species=:parasitoid),
    )

    trophic = CustomRule(:trophic, (sys, w, day, p) -> begin
        cas = sys[:cassava].population
        mb  = sys[:mealybug].population
        el  = sys[:parasitoid].population

        # Plant supply and mealybug acquisition (eqns 2–5)
        leaf = total_population(cas)
        P = photosynthate(leaf, w.radiation)
        b = mealybug_demand(mb, degree_days(cm_dev, w.T_mean))
        A = b > 0 ? acquire(cm_feeding, P, b) : 0.0
        φ = b > 0 ? A / b : 1.0

        # Plant growth: replace leaves that aged out or died, store the rest
        canopy = cas.stages[1].delay
        dd_cas = degree_days(cassava_dev, w.T_mean)
        replace = canopy.W[end] * canopy.k / canopy.τ * dd_cas + cas.stages[1].μ * dd_cas * leaf
        surplus = max(P - A, 0.0)
        new_leaf = min(surplus, replace)
        inject!(sys, :cassava, 1, new_leaf)
        sys[:tuber].population.value[] += surplus - new_leaf

        # Births scaled by φ, then food-related survival φ (eqn 6)
        births = φ * mealybug_max_births(mb)
        if φ < 1
            remove_fraction!(sys, :mealybug, 1 - φ)
            remove_fraction!(sys, :parasitoid, 1, 1 - φ)   # parasitoids inside starving hosts
        end
        inject!(sys, :mealybug, 1, births)

        # Parasitism by E. lopezi (eqns 7–13)
        attacked = 0.0
        parasitism_rate = 0.0
        if introduce_parasitoid && day >= introduction_day
            FEM = delay_total(el.stages[3].delay)
            dd_el = degree_days(el_dev, w.T_mean)
            bE = EL_OVIPOSITION * dd_el * el_oviposition_temperature(dd_el) * FEM /
                 (1 - EL_HOST_FEEDING)
            n_imm = delay_total(mb.stages[3].delay)
            n_ad = delay_total(mb.stages[4].delay)
            Hw = EL_PREF_IMMATURE * n_imm + EL_PREF_ADULT * n_ad
            if bE > 0
                attacked = parasitoid_attacks(Hw, FEM, bE)
                if attacked > 0
                    f = attacked / Hw
                    remove_fraction!(sys, :mealybug, 3, clamp(EL_PREF_IMMATURE * f, 0.0, 1.0))
                    remove_fraction!(sys, :mealybug, 4, clamp(EL_PREF_ADULT * f, 0.0, 1.0))
                    sex_ratio = clamp(Hw / (n_imm + n_ad), 0.3, 0.75)
                    inject!(sys, :parasitoid, 1, sex_ratio * (1 - EL_HOST_FEEDING) * attacked)
                    parasitism_rate = attacked / (n_imm + n_ad)
                end
                # Food-related survival and emigration of adults: hosts attacked / demand
                φE = min(1.0, attacked / bE)
                φE < 1 && remove_fraction!(sys, :parasitoid, 3, 1 - φE)
            end
        end
        return (supply=P, demand=b, acquired=A, φ=φ, births=births,
                attacked=attacked, parasitism_rate=parasitism_rate)
    end)

    events = AbstractScheduledEvent[]
    if introduce_parasitoid
        push!(events, SingleDayRelease(:parasitoid, initial_parasitoids, introduction_day;
                                       stage_idx=3))
    end

    prob = PBDMProblem(MultiSpeciesPBDMNew(), sys, weather, (1, n_days);
                       rules=AbstractInteractionRule[trophic], events=events)
    sol = solve(prob, DirectIteration())

    cm_stages = permutedims(sol.component_stage_totals[:mealybug])   # day × stage
    el_stages = permutedims(sol.component_stage_totals[:parasitoid])
    log = sol.rule_log[:trophic]
    return (; cm_stages, el_stages,
            cm_active = cm_stages[:, 3] .+ cm_stages[:, 4],     # immatures + adults
            el_total = vec(sum(el_stages, dims=2)),
            tuber = sol.component_totals[:tuber],
            leaf = sol.component_totals[:cassava],
            φ = [l.φ for l in log],
            parasitism_rate = [l.parasitism_rate for l in log],
            sol)
end

Key Insights

  1. Food limits the mealybug. Without natural enemies, the mealybug grows from one ovisac per plant until its demand outstrips what it can take from the plant’s photosynthate. φ then falls below 1, and fecundity and survival fall with it. Gutierrez et al. (1988b, p. 930) report a food-limited maximum of about 55 000 mealybugs per plant when rain-induced mortality is excluded, and about 10 000 per plant in the 1983–84 rainy-season runs.

  2. Classical biocontrol. Gutierrez et al. (1988b, p. 929) found that 0.5 E. lopezi females per plant suppressed the simulated mealybug to about 0.1 of its density without parasitism. Here the parasitoid persists through years 2–3 and holds the mealybug at a small fraction of its food-limited level. Its own numbers are regulated by the ratio of hosts attacked to demand.

  3. Physiological time. The parasitoid’s generation (egg to adult, 186 DD above 13.3 °C) is shorter than the mealybug’s (embryo to adult, 371 DD above 13.5 °C), so it completes about two generations per mealybug generation.

  4. Temperature. In this model, warmer weather weakens biocontrol. Above about 23 °C, the oviposition scalar $1 - [(\Delta t - 10)/10]^2$ falls, and it reaches zero at 33.3 °C. Cooler weather strengthens biocontrol.

  5. Bottom-up and top-down. The supply/demand ratio limits the mealybug from below and the parasitoid limits it from above. Because the mealybug takes photosynthate before the tubers do, biocontrol shows up as extra tuber dry matter.

Simplifications

  • The cassava plant is a canopy of constant size plus a tuber store, driven by light only. Water, nitrogen and the full plant demand model of Gutierrez et al. (1988a) are omitted. The nitrogen scalar ν on fecundity (eqn 2) is therefore 1.
  • Mealybug mass is not tracked separately. Demand uses the maximum mass at each age (the integral of the growth demand), so φ does not slow growth in mass. Embryos are a stage of their own, with the adult mortality rate, instead of being carried inside their mothers. Embryo shedding is omitted.
  • Parasitized mealybugs are removed at once, and their feeding stops. In the paper, parasitized mealybugs keep feeding until they mummify.
  • There is no rainfall, so rain-induced mortality (eqn 16) is absent. The coccinellid predators are omitted.
